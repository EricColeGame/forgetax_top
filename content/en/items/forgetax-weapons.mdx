## What Makes Forgetax Weapons Worth Keeping

Not every drop deserves inventory space. The players who progress fastest are usually the ones who filter hardest, and they apply the same question to every item: does this do something my current setup cannot?

A forgetax weapon earns a permanent slot when it fills a gap — a damage type you lack, a range band you can't cover, a utility effect your build depends on. Everything else belongs in a "test later" pile, not in your upgrade queue.

Most forgetax weapons fall into a handful of recognizable archetypes. Knowing which one you're holding tells you more about its real value than the numbers on the tooltip.

| Archetype | What it does well | Where it falls short | Pairs best with |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fast, low-commitment | Rapid hits, quick recovery, forgiving timing | Low per-hit damage, struggles against armored targets | On-hit effects, status stacking |
| Heavy, high-commitment | Massive single hits, breaks guards | Long wind-up, punishes whiffs hard | Stagger tools, burst windows |
| Ranged or charged | Safe damage from distance | Weak up close, demands resource management | Crowd control, positioning tools |
| Hybrid or utility | Buffs, debuffs, mobility, flexibility | Average raw output | Support builds, co-op roles |
| Scaling late-bloomer | Best-in-class ceiling once invested | Underwhelming until fully upgraded | Long-term main builds |

Three rules follow from that table:

- If you can't name the situation where you'd swap to it, you don't need it.
- A weapon that's only strong when maxed is a project, not a solution.
- For most players, versatility beats peak damage most of the time.

## How to Judge Forgetax Weapons Before You Invest

Tooltips lie by omission. They show you numbers, but they rarely show you how those numbers behave in motion. Before you commit materials to any forgetax weapon, run it through the same checklist.

| What to check | Why it matters | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Base damage | Sets the floor for every upgrade you'll ever add | Far below what you're already using |
| Scaling stat | Determines how the weapon grows with your build | Scales off a stat you never invest in |
| Attack speed and timing | Controls real output, not paper output | Great numbers, unusable rhythm |
| Range and hitbox | Decides whether you actually land hits | Ideal range that doesn't fit your play |
| Utility effects | Status, buffs, resource generation, crowd control | Nothing beyond raw damage |
| Upgrade ceiling | How far it can carry you before it plateaus | Caps out early with no payoff |
| Equip and upgrade cost | Opportunity cost against the rest of your build | Consumes a slot you need elsewhere |

The single most common trap is paper damage. A weapon that hits twice as hard but swings half as often is a lateral move at best, and it usually feels worse because every miss costs twice as much. Test the rhythm before you trust the math.

Player experience shared across forums and community servers tends to converge on the same point: the weapons people regret upgrading are almost never the ones with low numbers. They're the ones that felt wrong in the hand.

## Upgrade Priorities: Where Your Materials Should Go

Materials are the real currency in any item-driven game, and forgetax weapons compete for them against every other slot in your build. Spending in the right order is the difference between having one great weapon and having five mediocre ones.

| Stage | Typical goal | When it's worth doing |
|---|---|---|
| Making it usable | Bring base stats up to your current content level | Always, if you plan to test it properly |
| Unlocking the signature trait | Turns a stat stick into a build piece | Only if the trait fits your build |
| Refining secondary stats | Squeezes out the last few percent | Only on a confirmed keeper |
| Maxing out | Final gains at the highest cost | Only for your main weapon |

A few habits keep that table honest:

- Upgrade one forgetax weapon at a time. Splitting materials across three projects leaves you with three unfinished weapons.
- Never refine before you've tested. Refinement is the most expensive stage and the least reversible.
- Keep a "maybe" pile. Some weapons only become good when a new item or build option arrives.
- Write down why you're upgrading something. If you can't, you're collecting, not building.

## Matching Forgetax Weapons to Your Build

A weapon is only as good as the build around it. Two players can hold identical forgetax weapons and get completely different results, because synergy multiplies stats while mismatches divide them.

| Build style | Priority traits | What to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Crit or burst | High base damage, crit scaling, short damage windows | Chip-damage weapons with low crit value |
| Sustained damage | Attack speed, on-hit effects, low cost per swing | Slow wind-ups that break your rhythm |
| Control or utility | Status application, area coverage, cooldowns | Pure damage rolls with no utility |
| Bruiser or tank | Stagger, guard break, sustain effects | Fragile high-risk options |
| Hybrid or co-op | Buffs, debuffs, flexible range | One-dimensional picks |

The practical takeaway: match the weapon to the job, not to the tier list. A mid-tier weapon that fits your build will outperform a top-tier weapon that fights it every step of the way.

## The Testing Routine That Saves Materials

Guessing is expensive. A short, repeatable test cycle will tell you more in ten minutes than an hour of reading opinions online.

1. **Pick a fixed target.** Use a training dummy, a practice range, or a repeatable encounter so your results are comparable.
2. **Run a timed window.** Measure output over a consistent duration rather than a single burst, so you capture reloads, cooldowns, and recovery frames.
3. **Change one variable at a time.** Swap the weapon, not the weapon plus three build pieces. Otherwise you learn nothing.
4. **Take it into real content.** A weapon that wins on a dummy can still lose against moving targets and pressure.
5. **Record the result.** A simple note with the weapon, the setup, and the outcome prevents you from re-testing the same item twice.
6. **Decide and move on.** Keep, vault, or dismantle. Indecision is its own material cost.

## Common Mistakes With Forgetax Weapons

Most wasted resources come from a small set of repeatable errors. Fixing them costs nothing.

| Mistake | What it costs you | Better approach |

## FAQ

**What are forgetax weapons actually good for?**
They're best used as specialized tools rather than universal answers. Forgetax weapons tend to shine when they cover a specific gap — a damage type, a range band, or a utility effect — and underperform when you force them into a role they weren't built for.

**How many should I keep at once?**
A workable rule is one main weapon, one backup that covers your main's weakness, and a small "maybe" pile of two or three items. Anything beyond that is usually inventory clutter rather than optionality.

**Should I upgrade before or after my build is finished?**
After the core of your build is stable. Upgrading a weapon into an unfinished build means re-testing it later anyway, which doubles your material spend for the same result.

**How do I know if a drop is a keeper?**
Test it, don't guess. If it clears your current content comfortably, fits your scaling stat, and solves a problem you actually have, it's a keeper. If you have to invent a reason to use it, it isn't.
